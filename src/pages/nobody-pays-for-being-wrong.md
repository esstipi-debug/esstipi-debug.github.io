When a system is wrong after go-live, the cost usually lands in a generic account, weeks later, with nobody's name on it.

That one fact feeds two habits that look like opposites. A supervisor says "the system already calculated it" and obeys against the evidence. A picker ships a dented unit to protect a pick rate. One passes for faith in technology, the other for resistance to change. My reading: both are cheap for the person doing them, because neither pays for being wrong, and neither does the team that deployed the system, which has moved on by then.

The bestiary borrows its cast from Nietzsche's camel, lion and child, adds a scribe and a smith, and ends with a field guide to tell them apart.
