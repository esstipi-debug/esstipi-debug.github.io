Sometimes. In a study of more than 60,000 forecasts from four supply-chain companies, large adjustments helped most and small ones often made the forecast worse. The bigger problem is that, without a name and a recorded reason, a company cannot tell the planner who knew something from the one who was guessing, so it learns from neither.

## How it works

An override can be the one thing nobody else in the room knew: a customer about to cancel, a promotion nobody loaded. Or it can be a mood with a keyboard. From the outside the two look the same: a change with no name and no reason, corrected or not in a generic account weeks later.

Two cheap rules change that. Make the statistical forecast the default, so that changing it takes an explicit request. And ask for a recorded reason before any change. A reason field that accepts "as discussed" is a checkbox, not a reason.

## Evidence

Robert Fildes, Paul Goodwin, Michael Lawrence and Konstantinos Nikolopoulos examined more than 60,000 forecasts and outcomes from four supply-chain companies. In three of the four, judgmental adjustments improved accuracy on average. Large adjustments helped most; small ones often made the forecast worse.

In a forecasting experiment, Paul Goodwin tested cheap changes. Two of them worked: making the statistical forecast the default, so that changing it took an explicit request, and asking for a recorded reason before any change. Both improved accuracy.

## What this does not prove

- Fildes's data came from four companies' forecasts, and Goodwin's from an experiment. Neither is a warehouse after go-live.
- That a company learns from neither without a reason is my reading, not a finding of either study.
